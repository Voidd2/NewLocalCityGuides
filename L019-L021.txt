LEESTEKST (EN)
A Protestant church designed from the beginning
The Marekerk makes the architectural effects of the Reformation visible.
Leiden's medieval churches had been designed for Catholic worship.
After the Reformation, Protestants could remove altars, change furniture and install pulpits.
But the basic architecture remained medieval.
The Marekerk was different.
It could be planned specifically for Reformed worship.
The 1639 design
In 1639, Leiden's city architect Arent van 's-Gravesande produced the first design.
The Marekerk states that his teacher Jacob van Campen made several modifications.
Construction took roughly a decade and the church was completed in 1649. De Marekerk
Van 's-Gravesande worked within the developing Dutch Classical tradition.
That architectural language favoured symmetry, proportion and Classical order.
It provides an immediate contrast with the Gothic complexity of the Hooglandse Kerk.
Octagonal central planning
The national monument record describes the Marekerk as an eight-sided central church building dating from 1639–1649. Monumenten.nl
The plan reflects Reformed worship.
In Protestant churches, preaching and scripture became especially important.
The congregation gathered around the spoken Word.
A centralised plan could therefore serve the religious function more effectively than a long medieval basilica.
The heritage history of South Holland explicitly identifies the Marekerk as an early central church in the tradition of the Reformation. Geschiedenis van Zuid-Holland
Sight and sound
Religious priorities become practical architecture.
If the sermon matters, people need to hear it.
If the minister is central, people should be able to see him.
Large columns should not unnecessarily obstruct the view.
Seats should relate logically to the pulpit.
In that sense, the Marekerk addresses problems that a modern theatre designer would recognise.
The 1649 pulpit
The pulpit dates from 1649.
According to the Marekerk, it was modelled on the pulpit of Haarlem's Nieuwe Kerk. De Marekerk
It is therefore more than furniture.
It reveals the religious hierarchy of the room.
The dome
Above the central space rises the large dome.
Externally, it is one of Leiden's distinctive skyline features.
Internally, it unifies the octagonal space and draws attention toward the centre rather than toward a distant eastern altar.
Dutch Classicism
The Marekerk belongs to the tradition of Dutch Classicism.
Classical architectural ideas — proportion, order and clear geometry — replace much of the visual complexity associated with late medieval Gothic buildings.
Visitors can understand the distinction more easily by visiting L019 first.
The difference is immediately visible without requiring specialist architectural vocabulary.
Jacob van Campen's portal
Jacob van Campen influenced the early design.
He also left a later, clearly documented contribution.
The monumental portal was added in 1659 to his design. Monumenten.nl
The entrance is therefore ten years younger than the completed church itself.
An organ older than the building
The organ creates another historical layer.
Its historic upper section originated around 1560, when Peter de Swart built it as a choir organ for the Pieterskerk.
It was enlarged in 1629.
In 1733, it moved to the Marekerk.
Rudolph Garrels enlarged it again in 1735. Monumenten.nl
A church designed specifically for Protestant worship therefore contains an instrument from an older phase of Leiden's religious architecture.
Still an active congregation
The Marekerk remains a working Protestant church.
The Marekerkgemeente currently holds Sunday services at 10:00 and 17:00. De Marekerk
That matters for visitors.
They are entering a functioning religious space, not only a heritage attraction.
