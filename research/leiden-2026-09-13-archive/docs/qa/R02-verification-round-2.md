## 1. L010 — THE TULIP THEFT IS REAL, DATED, AND BETTER THAN EXPECTED

`L010-R01` flagged the "first tulip bulbs from Sultan Suleyman" claim as chronologically
impossible, and recorded the theft as undocumented. Both are now resolved.

### The Suleyman claim — real, but indirect
**Ogier Ghiselin de Busbecq**, Flemish ambassador of the Habsburg Empire **at Suleyman's court**,
sent tulip bulbs and seeds to his network in Europe **in the 1560s**. One recipient was
**Clusius**, then working in **Vienna**. In **1593**, appointed prefect at Leiden, **Clusius
brought his tulip collection with him** and planted it in the new garden.

So the Ottoman route is genuine; the direct-gift-from-the-Sultan version is not. **The
permitted formulation:** the bulbs reached Clusius in Vienna in the 1560s through the Habsburg
ambassador to Suleyman's court, and he carried them to Leiden thirty years later.

### The theft — documented, dated, and it explains a place name
- **Clusius refused to sell.** He regarded the tulips as **scientific objects** and would not
  deal with commercial parties.
- **His refusal had an unintended consequence: in the night of 1596 to 1597, his bulbs were
  stolen from the Hortus.**
- **The thief sold them to farmers in the region north of Leiden — the area still called the
  Bollenstreek, the bulb region, today.**

**This is the strongest single fact produced by any research pass in this project.** It is
dated, it has a motive, it has a consequence, and **the consequence is a place name that still
exists on the map**. A professor tried to keep tulips out of trade; the theft put them into it;
and the Dutch bulb industry grew where the stolen bulbs were sold.

⚠️ `R02` round 3 must find an **institutional** source for the 1596/97 date before publication —
this is exactly the kind of too-good-to-check story DEC-013 exists for. Leiden University's
Clusius correspondence is the place to look.

## 2. L001 — THE WATER SYSTEM IS DATED

`L001-R01` recorded the water system as "probable, dating unknown, almost certainly post-1651".
Confirmed and now precise:

- **In 1692/93 a fountain was realised on the Vismarkt, and water reservoirs were laid out on
  the Burchtheuvel to supply it.**
- The water came from the **Burchtgrachtje**, **filtered by a large water cellar in the foot of
  the hill**.
- This matches the **Visfontein's own date of 1693** exactly. The two are one project.

Also confirmed: **shortly after the city bought the Burcht in 1651**, a new access was made in
the **Nieuwstraat** — **a house had to be demolished** to build the sandstone gate. And the
**Heerenlogement** on the Burcht grounds is corroborated by a separate Erfgoed Leiden story
about its façade stone.

**Erfgoed Leiden has a "Vondst van de week" entry titled *Waterkelders ontdekt*** — the water
cellars were archaeologically found. That is the primary lead for round 3.

**Effect on the product:** the L001→L002 connection is no longer "probable". It is a dated,
single, deliberate 1692/93 municipal engineering project, and both stops can now say so.

## 3. L006 — A WARNING NINE DAYS BEFORE

`L006-R01` asked for a contemporary account of the 1512 collapse and a cause. The cause is still
open, but the sequence is now much sharper:

> **On 25 February 1512, during a sermon in the Pieterskerk, the congregation was startled by
> large pieces of stone falling with thunderous force.**
> **In the night of 4–5 March 1512, the tower came down.**

**There was a warning, in public, nine days before.** People were sitting in church when the
building began to shed stone.

One account places the collapse **during a severe storm**. The cause is **not established**, and
must not be stated as if it were.

⚠️ Still not found: a **contemporary** chronicle. Everything so far is a modern account. Round 3
should go to Erfgoed Leiden's church and parish archives.

**Height:** "the hundred-metre-high tower" is consistent with the 100–110 m range in `R01`.
Still needs an institutional source before publication.
