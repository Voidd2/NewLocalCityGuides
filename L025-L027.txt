## VOICE-OVER SCRIPT (EN)

Enter the central hall.

Then stop.

Because standing inside a museum in the Dutch city of Leiden is an entire building created thousands of kilometres away...

more than two thousand years ago.

The **Temple of Taffeh**.

Not a replica.

A genuine temple from Nubia, reconstructed from **657 sandstone blocks**.

It dates roughly from **25 BC to AD 25**, when Egypt formed part of the Roman Empire. It may originally have been dedicated to the goddess Isis.

But the story of this museum does not begin in Egypt.

It begins in Leiden.

In **1744**, Leiden University inherited around 150 Greek and Roman sculptures from the collection of Gerard van Papenbroek.

That became one of the foundations of a much larger collection.

On **13 June 1818**, the twenty-five-year-old **Caspar Reuvens** was appointed professor of archaeology and director of Leiden University's archaeological cabinet.

That date is regarded as the foundation of the Rijksmuseum van Oudheden.

Leiden University describes Reuvens as the **world's first professor of archaeology**.

That appointment marks an important change.

Ancient objects were no longer valuable only because they were beautiful or rare.

Researchers increasingly asked:

Where was this found?

What surrounded it?

What layer of soil did it come from?

What can its context tell us?

Archaeology was becoming a scientific discipline.

The museum moved several times.

Its first public display opened on Houtstraat in **1821**.

The collection moved to Breestraat in 1838.

By around **1920**, the antiquities had been transferred to the present complex at Rapenburg 28.

Then came the Temple of Taffeh.

During the 1960s, construction of the Aswan High Dam threatened archaeological sites across Nubia.

UNESCO coordinated an international campaign to document and rescue monuments.

The Netherlands participated.

Egypt subsequently gave the temple to the Netherlands.

By **1969**, transport crates containing the dismantled monument had arrived at the museum.

The former courtyard was eventually roofed over to house it.

By the end of **1979**, the new central hall was complete.

Yet the temple had already lived several lives.

It belonged to a world of Egyptian religion under Roman rule.

In **AD 710**, it became a Christian church.

Centuries later, people and livestock used the former sacred building as shelter.

Walk around it.

Look at the individual blocks.

This is not one frozen moment called “Ancient Egypt”.

It is a building that survived changing religions, empires, communities, archaeology, international politics and finally museum display.

That is why the museum's deeper story is not simply about antiquity.

It is also about a much more complicated question:

**How does the past end up in front of us?**

# LEESTEKST (NL) – Uitgebreidere versie

## Van universiteitskast naar nationaal archeologiemuseum

Het Rijksmuseum van Oudheden is nauw verweven met de geschiedenis van de Universiteit Leiden.

De collectie telt tegenwoordig ongeveer **150.000 voorwerpen**, maar begon veel kleiner.

In **1744** kreeg de universiteit ongeveer 150 Griekse en Romeinse sculpturen uit de erfenis van Gerard van Papenbroek.

Die beelden waren nog geen modern archeologisch museum.

Ze waren onderdeel van een oudere Europese verzamelcultuur waarin antiquiteiten werden gekocht, geërfd, bestudeerd en tentoongesteld.

### 1818: Caspar Reuvens

De grote institutionele omslag kwam op **13 juni 1818**.

Caspar Reuvens werd benoemd tot hoogleraar archeologie en directeur van het *Archaeologisch Cabinet der Hoogeschool*.

Het RMO beschouwt die datum als zijn stichtingsdatum.

Universiteit Leiden noemt Reuvens de eerste hoogleraar archeologie ter wereld.

Hij was slechts 25 jaar oud.

Dat zegt veel over het vroege karakter van archeologie als zelfstandige discipline.

Aan het begin van de negentiende eeuw bestond de moderne archeologie zoals wij die kennen nog nauwelijks.

Reuvens werkte juist mee aan de ontwikkeling van een systematischere benadering van vondsten en hun context.

### Context wordt belangrijk

Een archeologisch object zonder vindplaatsinformatie vertelt minder dan hetzelfde object mét context.

Daarom zijn vragen als deze cruciaal:

Waar lag het?

Hoe diep?

Met welke andere voorwerpen?

Is er een nederzetting?

Een graf?

Een tempel?

Een afvalkuil?

Reuvens legde bij Nederlandse opgravingen sterkere nadruk op registratie en documentatie.

Tussen **1827 en 1834** leidde hij bijvoorbeeld groot onderzoek naar de Romeinse nederzetting Forum Hadriani bij Voorburg. Universiteit Leiden noemt dit de eerste grote moderne archeologische opgraving van Nederland.

### Eerste museumruimte

De archeologische verzameling had aanvankelijk geen groot museumgebouw.

In **1821** opende de eerste presentatie in een eenvoudig pand aan de Houtstraat.

De collectie groeide snel.

In **1838** verhuisde ze naar Breestraat.

Zelfs daar ontstond opnieuw ruimtegebrek.

### Rapenburg 28

Toen het natuurhistorische museum na 1918 uit het complex aan het Rapenburg vertrok, kwam er ruimte vrij.

In **1920** waren de archeologische collecties naar het huidige complex overgebracht.

Daarmee keerde het museum terug naar een omgeving die al aan zijn vroege geschiedenis verbonden was.

### Wat verzamelt het museum?

De vaste collecties omvatten tegenwoordig grote verzamelgebieden:

- Egypte en Nubië;
- Griekse, Romeinse en Etruskische oudheden;
- het oude Nabije Oosten;
- archeologie van Nederland, van prehistorie tot middeleeuwen.

Dat laatste is belangrijk.

Een archeologiemuseum gaat niet alleen over “verre oude beschavingen”.

De Nederlandse bodem is zelf één groot archeologisch archief.

### De Tempel van Taffeh

Toch is het grootste afzonderlijke object onmogelijk te missen.

De Tempel van Taffeh meet ongeveer **4,5 bij 8,25 bij 6,3 meter** en bestaat uit 657 stenen blokken.

Hij stond oorspronkelijk bij Taphis in Nubië en dateert uit de Romeinse keizertijd, circa **25 v.Chr.–25 n.Chr.**

De bouwstijl is traditioneel Egyptisch.

De tempel was mogelijk verbonden met de cultus van Isis.

Dat is een mooi voorbeeld van hoe culturele identiteit in de oudheid ingewikkelder is dan eenvoudige labels.

Politiek gezien stond de streek onder Romeinse macht.

Architectonisch en religieus was veel duidelijk Egyptisch.

### De tempel verandert van functie

De geschiedenis stopte niet toen de oorspronkelijke eredienst verdween.

In **710** werd het gebouw gebruikt als christelijke kerk.

Vanaf ongeveer de dertiende eeuw gebruikten Nubische bewoners de voormalige tempel vooral als verblijf voor mensen en dieren.

Daarmee is het geen tijdcapsule.

Het is een gebouw dat werd aangepast aan nieuwe gemeenschappen.

### De Aswandam

In de twintigste eeuw veranderde het landschap opnieuw.

Door de bouw van de Aswan High Dam zou een groot gebied in Nubië onder water lopen.

UNESCO organiseerde een internationale campagne om bedreigd erfgoed te redden.

Verschillende landen leverden geld, expertise en archeologisch werk.

Nederland behoorde daartoe.

Als erkenning werd de Tempel van Taffeh aan Nederland geschonken.

De collectiegegevens registreren de schenking in **1969**; dat jaar stond de museumhof al vol transportkisten met de 657 blokken.

### Een museum wordt verbouwd voor één object

Een complete tempel zet je niet in een gewone museumzaal.

Daarom werd de voormalige binnenplaats van het museum overdekt.

Eind **1979** was de nieuwe centrale Tempelzaal gereed.

Dat maakt de Tempelzaal zelf interessant:

het moderne museumgebouw werd aangepast om een oud gebouw binnen een ander gebouw te kunnen tonen.

### Gratis te zien

Dus ook een toerist met weinig tijd of budget kan de tempel zien.

Dat zou ik expliciet als route-tip opnemen.

### Niet elk object kwam via moderne archeologie binnen

Bij dit museum moet ook historische verzamelcontext worden genoemd.

De collectie groeide deels door opgravingen, maar ook door:

aankopen,

schenkingen,

verzamelingen,

negentiende-eeuwse internationale netwerken

en objecten die in koloniale verhoudingen naar Nederland kwamen.

Onderzoek naar de koloniale en slavernijgeschiedenis van Leiden laat bijvoorbeeld zien dat Javaanse oudheden in de negentiende eeuw via Nederlandse koloniale bestuurders naar de collectie kwamen.

Dat betekent niet dat de volledige RMO-collectie als één type verzameling moet worden behandeld.

Het betekent wel dat “hoe kwam dit hier?” ook hier een relevante museumvraag is.

## LEESTEKST (EN)

## From university collection to national museum

The Rijksmuseum van Oudheden grew directly out of Leiden University's collections.

Today it holds approximately **150,000 objects**, but one early foundation was much smaller: around 150 Greek and Roman sculptures inherited by the university from Gerard van Papenbroek in **1744**.

The decisive institutional moment came on **13 June 1818**, when Caspar Reuvens became professor of archaeology and director of the university's Archaeological Cabinet.

The museum treats this as its foundation date.

Leiden University describes Reuvens as the first professor of archaeology anywhere in the world.

Archaeology was only beginning to develop into a distinct academic discipline.

Reuvens promoted more systematic documentation.

An object became more useful when researchers also knew where it had been found, what surrounded it and how the archaeological layers related to one another.

Between **1827 and 1834**, he directed major excavations at the Roman settlement of Forum Hadriani near modern Voorburg. Leiden University describes this as the first major modern archaeological excavation in the Netherlands.

### Moving collections

The first public presentation opened on Houtstraat in **1821**.

Growing collections forced a move to Breestraat in 1838.

After the natural-history museum left the Rapenburg complex, the antiquities collections were moved there; by **1920**, the transfer was complete.

### What does the museum collect?

The major areas now include ancient Egypt and Nubia, the Classical world, the ancient Near East and archaeology from the Netherlands.

That range matters.

Archaeology is not simply the study of distant monumental civilizations.

Dutch landscapes and cities also contain thousands of years of archaeological evidence.

### The Temple of Taffeh

The largest single object is the Temple of Taffeh.

The building measures approximately 4.5 by 8.25 by 6.3 metres and consists of **657 sandstone blocks**.

It was built in Nubia during the Roman imperial period, around **25 BC–AD 25**.

The architecture is traditionally Egyptian.

The temple may have been connected with the cult of Isis.

This complicates simple historical categories: politically, Egypt and Lower Nubia existed within the Roman imperial system, while local religious and architectural traditions remained powerful.

### Several lives

The temple was repeatedly adapted.

In **AD 710**, it became a Christian church.

By later medieval centuries, people used the former temple as accommodation for humans and livestock.

The building was therefore never frozen in a single historical moment.

### The Nubian rescue campaign

In the twentieth century, the construction of the Aswan High Dam threatened numerous archaeological sites in Nubia.

UNESCO organised a large international campaign to save and document threatened monuments.

The Netherlands participated.

Egypt subsequently presented the Temple of Taffeh to the Netherlands.

The museum's collection record dates the donation to **1969**, when crates containing the temple's 657 blocks were already occupying the museum courtyard.

### Building a hall around a temple

A full ancient temple obviously required more than an ordinary gallery.

The former museum courtyard was roofed over and transformed into the present central hall.

The project was completed at the end of **1979**.

A modern museum building had effectively been redesigned around an ancient building.

### You can see it for free

An especially useful visitor fact:

the Temple Hall is **free to enter even without a museum ticket**.

The café, shop and other entrance facilities are also located in this publicly accessible area.

That makes L025 useful even for visitors who do not have time for the full museum.

### How collections were formed

The collection did not grow only through modern scientific excavation.

Like other nineteenth-century European museums, it also expanded through gifts, purchases and international collecting networks.

Research into Leiden's colonial history has shown that some Javanese antiquities reached the museum through Dutch colonial structures.

That does not mean every object shares the same history.

It means provenance should be treated as a legitimate historical question.

### Visiting in 2026

The museum is open **daily from 10:00 to 17:00**, with additional Thursday evening opening from **19:00 to 22:00**.

It is closed on several days including **3 October**, Leiden's annual Relief celebration.

Adult admission is **€14 including the audio guide**.

Visitors aged eighteen and under enter free.

The Dutch Museum Card and eligible student cards provide free admission.

### The central question

Rather than presenting L025 simply as a collection of spectacular antiquities, the app should ask:

**How does knowledge about the ancient past reach us?**

Excavation.

Documentation.

Collection.

International cooperation.

Political decisions.

And sometimes the literal movement of entire monuments.

The Temple of Taffeh makes that history impossible to ignore.

## A science museum inside a place where medicine was actually taught

Rijksmuseum Boerhaave occupies a location that already formed part of medical history centuries before the museum existed.

The building is the former **Caecilia Hospital**, where patients received treatment and medical students received practical instruction.

### The Saint Caecilia Convent

The site's documented history begins in **1414**, when the Saint Caecilia Convent was founded.

Augustinian sisters cared for sick and elderly people as part of their charitable religious obligations.

Medieval healthcare did not operate through modern hospitals, insurance systems and specialised institutions.

Religious charity, poor relief and civic institutions often overlapped.

### After the Reformation

When Leiden became Protestant, former Catholic institutional property passed into municipal hands.

Responsibility for social welfare increasingly shifted toward the city government.

The city's population grew rapidly after 1574.

More care capacity was needed.

### A hospital from around 1600

In **1596**, the city decided to use the former convent as an additional hospital.

By around **1600**, the facility was functioning.

Museum research describes two large upstairs wards, each containing around twenty beds.

The ground floor included small cells for psychiatric patients.

Elderly residents also lived within the complex.

Categories that modern healthcare separates were therefore combined.

### Clinical teaching from 1636

From **1636**, Leiden University rented **twelve beds** for practical medical education.

This brought theory into direct contact with actual patients.

Students had to observe disease rather than simply memorise what respected ancient medical authorities had written.

That sounds obvious today.

It was historically significant.

### Herman Boerhaave

**Herman Boerhaave (1668–1738)** became one of the most internationally influential scholars associated with Leiden.

His work crossed medicine, botany and chemistry.

His teaching became particularly renowned for its systematic character.

As professor of practical medicine, he visited the Caecilia Hospital roughly **twice each week** with students.

The long-standing Leiden tradition of bedside education became especially famous during his career.

### An international reputation

Students travelled from across Europe to study in Leiden.

Through them, Leiden's medical methods and intellectual culture circulated internationally.

Boerhaave became so influential that contemporaries described him as a teacher of Europe.

### The patient's perspective

The success story requires an important qualification.

The Caecilia Hospital primarily cared for poor people.

Museum documentation explicitly states that some patients also served as teaching material for the medical faculty.

This does not justify inventing emotions or applying twenty-first-century medical procedures retroactively.

But the difference in power is historically relevant.

A university professor, a medical student and a poor hospital patient did not occupy equal social positions.

### End of the hospital

In **1799**, Leiden University decided to establish another academic hospital elsewhere.

Its founders were academics concerned with preserving Dutch scientific heritage.

The first director was August Crommelin, who worked at the Kamerlingh Onnes Laboratory.

### From scholarly collection to public museum

The early institution focused heavily on preserving collections for scholars.

During the twentieth century, its role broadened.

Scientific heritage increasingly became something to interpret for a general audience rather than only preserve for specialists.

### Return to the hospital

In **1991**, the museum moved into the former Caecilia Hospital.

That connection gives the site unusual depth.

Objects explaining the history of clinical medicine are displayed inside a place where clinical teaching itself had taken place.

A scientific instrument is not just an old machine.

It changes the limits of perception.

A microscope reveals structures invisible to the naked eye.

The museum should not be presented as a parade of flawless geniuses.

Scientific knowledge changes.

Experiments fail.

Hypotheses are revised.

One generation's certainty may become another generation's discarded theory.

That process of correction is not a weakness of science.

It is part of how science works.

## LEESTEKST (EN)

## A museum that also investigates its own history

Wereldmuseum Leiden is one of the more complex stops in the route because the meaning of the institution itself has changed.

A nineteenth-century European ethnographic museum understood collecting differently from a museum in 2026.

### Von Siebold and the founding collection

The official institutional history begins in **1837** with the creation of the **National Japanese Museum Von Siebold**.

Its core was Philipp Franz von Siebold's Japanese collection.

Von Siebold was a German physician working in Dutch service.

Between **1823 and 1830**, he worked from the Dutch trading post of Deshima near Nagasaki and collected a wide range of objects and information about Japan.

His collection was displayed at **Rapenburg 19**.

Today that building is the **SieboldHuis**, L028.

The next route location therefore begins where this museum's institutional history began.

### King William I and national collections

The museum's origins were also connected with King William I, who promoted national scientific and artistic institutions.

Scholars were commissioned to collect in places including China, Indonesia and Japan.

Objects from the Royal Cabinet of Curiosities later entered the Leiden collection after that institution closed in 1883.

### The National Ethnographic Museum

In **1864**, the museum was renamed the National Ethnographic Museum.

Its purpose had expanded far beyond a single Japanese collection.

It attempted to represent societies from many regions of the world.

### Colonial expansion of the collection

After Amsterdam's **International Colonial Exhibition of 1883**, significant material entered the Leiden collection.

The word “colonial” must remain visible in the interpretation.

These collections grew in a period when European states exercised military, political and economic control over large parts of the world.

That shaped who could collect, what could be removed and how cultures were represented.

### The Dutch East Indies

The museum itself records that so many objects arrived from the **Dutch East Indies** during the late nineteenth century that the institution struggled with space.

The collection's development therefore cannot be separated from the Netherlands' colonial presence in Indonesia.

### Not every object was looted

Historical accuracy requires caution.

It would be wrong to claim that the entire collection consists of stolen objects.

Wereldmuseum identifies several acquisition routes:

trade,

Christian mission,

war,

tourism,

scientific research

and donations.

Some objects were purchased or given.

Some have incomplete provenance.

Others were obtained through coercion or military conflict.

### Colonial injustice was nevertheless real

It would be equally misleading to reduce the story to neutral scientific collecting.

Wereldmuseum explicitly acknowledges that part of its wider collection was looted during the colonial period.

The institution conducts provenance research and actively participates in restitution processes.

Its wider collection records identify around 15,000 objects associated with military conflict, although the museum stresses that this cannot simply be treated as a complete count of all looted material.

### Provenance research

Provenance research reconstructs the history of ownership and transfer.

Researchers use catalogues, letters, acquisition documents, military records and knowledge from communities of origin.

Wereldmuseum participates in research programmes designed to improve methods for investigating colonial collections.

### Restitution

Dutch national policy now allows countries of origin to request the return of colonial objects in state collections.

The museum provides research; independent review and government decisions determine the outcome.

A museum object is therefore not necessarily permanently fixed in Leiden.

Research can alter its future.

### The former Academic Hospital

Meanwhile, the physical museum continued expanding.

The former Leiden Academic Hospital became available in **1931**.

The building was allocated to the museum in 1933, but the economic crisis delayed opening until **1937**.

The museum therefore occupies a site originally built for medicine rather than ethnography.

### War

In **1939**, staff began moving major objects to safer storage.

By 1944, the public collections had been fully evacuated.

On **11 December 1944**, bombing struck the grounds and one bomb hit the building, causing extensive glass damage.

Documentary images should be preferred for this section of the app.

### From Volkenkunde to Wereldmuseum

For generations, the institution was widely known as **Museum Volkenkunde**.

On **4 October 2023**, it joined the shared name **Wereldmuseum**.

The organisation associates the new identity with interconnected cultures, multiple perspectives and a more critical understanding of its own colonial history.

### A visitor exercise

The app could ask every visitor to select one object and investigate:

Who made it?

Where was it used?

When did it enter the museum?

Who collected it?

Under what circumstances?

Are parts of the provenance still unknown?

Unknown information should not automatically be filled with assumptions.

Sometimes the gap itself is historically important.

### The central question

The strongest question at this location is:

The maker?

The collector?

## LEESTEKST (DE)

## Ein Museum, das auch seine eigene Geschichte erforscht

Wereldmuseum Leiden gehört zu den komplexeren Stationen dieser Route.

Nicht weil die Daten unklar wären.

Sondern weil sich die Bedeutung des Museums selbst verändert hat.

### Von Siebold

Die institutionelle Geschichte beginnt **1837** mit dem **'s Rijks Japansch Museum Von Siebold**.

Die Ausgangssammlung stammte von Philipp Franz von Siebold.

Der deutsche Arzt arbeitete in niederländischen Diensten und hielt sich von **1823 bis 1830** in Japan auf.

Von der Handelsstation Deshima bei Nagasaki aus sammelte er zahlreiche Gegenstände und Informationen.

Seine Sammlung wurde am **Rapenburg 19** gezeigt.

Heute befindet sich dort das **SieboldHuis**, L028.

### König Willem I.

Auch König Willem I. spielte eine Rolle.

Er förderte nationale Kunst- und Wissenschaftssammlungen und ließ unter anderem in China, Indonesien und Japan sammeln.

Nach der Auflösung des Königlichen Raritätenkabinetts 1883 kamen weitere Objekte nach Leiden.

### 1864

**1864** erhielt die Einrichtung den Namen **'s Rijks Ethnographisch Museum**.

Aus einer Japan-Sammlung war ein Museum geworden, das Gesellschaften aus vielen Teilen der Welt darstellen wollte.

### Kolonialer Kontext

Nach der Internationalen Kolonialausstellung von **1883** wurden zahlreiche weitere Objekte übernommen.

Diese Entwicklung fand innerhalb europäischer Kolonialherrschaft statt.

Macht beeinflusste, wer sammeln durfte, welche Dinge bewegt wurden und wie andere Gesellschaften in Europa dargestellt wurden.

### Niederländisch-Indien

Ende des 19. Jahrhunderts kamen so viele Gegenstände aus **Niederländisch-Indien** in die Sammlung, dass erneut Platzmangel entstand.

Die Museumsgeschichte ist daher eng mit der niederländischen Kolonialgeschichte verbunden.

### Nicht alles ist Raubkunst

Es wäre falsch, jedes Objekt automatisch als geraubt zu bezeichnen.

Wereldmuseum nennt unterschiedliche Wege des Erwerbs:

Handel,

Mission,

Krieg,

Tourismus,

wissenschaftliche Forschung,

Schenkungen.

Die Geschichte muss für einzelne Objekte untersucht werden.

### Aber Raub fand statt

Ebenso falsch wäre eine rein neutrale Erzählung von wissenschaftlichem Sammeln.

Wereldmuseum erklärt selbst, dass **ein Teil seiner gemeinsamen Sammlung während der Kolonialzeit geraubt wurde**.

Das Museum betreibt deshalb Herkunftsforschung und unterstützt Verfahren zur möglichen Rückgabe.

### Herkunftsforschung

Forscher untersuchen:

alte Kataloge,

Kaufverträge,

Militärarchive,

Briefe,

Schenkungsunterlagen

und Informationen aus Herkunftsgemeinschaften.

Programme wie PPROCE entwickelten Methoden für diese Forschung gemeinsam mit anderen Institutionen.

### Rückgabe

Niederländische Politik ermöglicht heute Rückgabeanträge für koloniale Objekte der staatlichen Sammlung.

Das Museum liefert Forschungsergebnisse; über die tatsächliche Rückgabe entscheiden staatliche Verfahren.

### Das ehemalige Akademische Krankenhaus

Der heutige Standort wurde **1931** frei.

1933 erhielt das Museum den Gebäudekomplex, doch wegen der Wirtschaftskrise öffnete es erst **1937** für das Publikum.

Das heutige Museum befindet sich also in einem ehemaligen Krankenhaus.

### Zweiter Weltkrieg

Seit **1939** wurden wichtige Sammlungsstücke ausgelagert.

1944 wurde die öffentliche Sammlung vollständig geräumt.

Am **11. Dezember 1944** traf ein Bombenangriff das Gelände und beschädigte das Gebäude.

### Museum Volkenkunde → Wereldmuseum

Viele Generationen kannten die Einrichtung als **Museum Volkenkunde**.

Seit **4. Oktober 2023** lautet der gemeinsame Name **Wereldmuseum**.

Die Institution verbindet den Namen mit globalen Verflechtungen, mehreren Perspektiven und einer kritischeren Auseinandersetzung mit der eigenen Kolonialgeschichte.

## AANVULLENDE FEITEN L027

- **Oprichting:** **1837** als 's Rijks Japansch Museum Von Siebold.
- **Oorspronkelijke kern:** Japanverzameling van Philipp Franz von Siebold, verzameld tijdens zijn werk in Japan 1823–1830.
- **Eerste locatie:** Rapenburg 19, nu **L028 SieboldHuis**.
- **Nieuwe naam:** 's Rijks Ethnographisch Museum vanaf **1864**.
- **Uitbreiding 1883:** collecties Koninklijk Kabinet van Zeldzaamheden en objecten uit Internationale Koloniale Tentoonstelling.
- **Huidige gebouw:** voormalig Academisch Ziekenhuis.
- **Publieksopening op huidige locatie:** **1937**.
- **Oorlogsschade:** bombardement **11 december 1944**.
- **Langere historische naam:** Museum Volkenkunde.
- **Naam Wereldmuseum:** sinds **4 oktober 2023**.
- **Koloniale context:** museum erkent dat een deel van de gezamenlijke collectie tijdens koloniale periode geroofd is.
- **Herkomstonderzoek:** actief onderzoek en samenwerking rond restitutie.
- **Openingstijden 2026:** di–zo 10.00–17.00; geselecteerde vakantiemaandagen extra open.
- **Entree:** volwassenen €16 online / €18 kassa; 6–18 €6,50 / €8; 0–5 gratis; Museumkaart gratis.
- **Studenten:** €11; Universiteit Leiden gratis.
- **Adres:** Steenstraat 1, Leiden.
- **Beste moment:** minimaal 1,5–2 uur; goed te combineren met L028 om het Von Siebold-verhaal af te maken.
- **Connecties:** **L028 Japanmuseum SieboldHuis**, Universiteit Leiden, koloniale geschiedenis en voormalig Academisch Ziekenhuis.
