LEESTEKST (EN)
A seventeenth-century story in a 1987 building
Molen De Put is a useful lesson in historical accuracy.
The site is historic.
A mill genuinely stood here in the seventeenth century.
The present structure follows a historic mill type.
But the timber building you are looking at today is not an original seventeenth-century survivor.
The Dutch Mill Database gives its construction year as 1987. [Molendatabase](https://legacy.molendatabase.nl/nederland/molen.php?nummer=1027&utm_source=chatgpt.com)
That should not be hidden.
It should be part of the experience.
A rapidly growing city
Leiden expanded dramatically after the Relief of 1574.
Visit Leiden states that the population grew from roughly 10,000 shortly after the siege to around 24,000 by 1600.
The city became overcrowded and expanded on the northern side of the Rhine. [Visit Leiden](https://www.visitleiden.nl/nl/locaties/529766/molen-de-put?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
New fortifications provided useful elevated sites for windmills.
That was practical engineering.
Windmills require unobstructed wind.
A bastion above the surrounding buildings is a much better location than a narrow street.
Jan Jansz. Put
In 1619, miller Jan Jansz. Put built a wooden post mill on the bastion beside the Galgewater.
The present mill is named after him. [Molendatabase](https://legacy.molendatabase.nl/nederland/molen.php?nummer=1027&utm_source=chatgpt.com)
A post mill is one of the oldest European windmill designs.
Much of the wooden mill body pivots around a central vertical post so that the entire structure can be turned toward the wind.
This is fundamentally different from De Valk, where the stone tower remains fixed while the cap and sails rotate.
For visitors, the two Leiden windmills therefore show two very different engineering traditions.
Destruction and rebuilding
The 1619 mill burned down in 1640.
It was rebuilt. [Molendatabase](https://legacy.molendatabase.nl/nederland/molen.php?nummer=1027&utm_source=chatgpt.com)
Fire was a constant threat to working mills.
They combined timber construction, dry grain, airborne flour dust, friction and fast-moving machinery.
A larger mill takes over
In 1729, the post mill was replaced by a tall stage mill.
This successor was more comparable to De Valk and was known under names including De Korenmolen or De Koornbloem. [Molendatabase](https://legacy.molendatabase.nl/nederland/molen.php?nummer=1027&utm_source=chatgpt.com)
This reflects a wider development in urban milling.
As cities became denser, mills often had to become taller in order to reach free wind above rooftops.
Larger structures could also handle greater production.
Demolition
In 1817, the later mill was demolished.
No immediate replacement followed. [Molendatabase](https://legacy.molendatabase.nl/nederland/molen.php?nummer=1027&utm_source=chatgpt.com)
For more than 160 years, the historic milling location no longer looked like one.
Archaeology changes the site
In 1982, archaeologists discovered foundation remains associated with the former mill bastion. [Molendatabase](https://legacy.molendatabase.nl/nederland/molen.php?nummer=1027&utm_source=chatgpt.com)
The vanished landscape suddenly became tangible again.
Soon afterwards, an initiative developed to reconstruct a post mill at the location.
Support from residents helped make the plan possible.
Reconstruction in 1987
The present De Put was completed in 1987.
The Dutch Mill Database explicitly describes it as a replica of Jan Jansz. Put's wooden grain mill of 1619. [Molendatabase](https://legacy.molendatabase.nl/nederland/molen.php?nummer=1027&utm_source=chatgpt.com)
It is a closed post mill with sails spanning roughly 24 metres.
Inside is functional milling equipment.
This matters.
The project did not merely reproduce the appearance of a windmill.
It recreated a working machine.
Still capable of milling
The mill remains operational.
Volunteers use it to grind grain. [Molendatabase](https://legacy.molendatabase.nl/nederland/molen.php?nummer=1027&utm_source=chatgpt.com)
That gives visitors something a photograph or scale model cannot provide.
When the sails move, forces travel through the wooden structure exactly because the machine has a job to perform.
Ownership
The municipality originally owned the reconstructed mill.
Since 2016, it has belonged to Molenstichting Leiden en Omstreken, a foundation dedicated to preserving Leiden-area windmills. [Molendatabase](https://legacy.molendatabase.nl/nederland/molen.php?nummer=1027&utm_source=chatgpt.com)
Rembrandt: the right connection and the wrong connection
The temptation is to present De Put as “Rembrandt's windmill”.
It was not.
The current mill was built centuries after his death.
However, its predecessor from 1619 did exist during Rembrandt's childhood in Leiden.
And Rembrandt's father was himself a miller.
That creates a legitimate historical connection.
The mill helps visitors imagine the kind of economic landscape in which Rembrandt grew up.
The Rembrandt Bridge
The wooden bridge beside De Put is today called the Rembrandtbrug.
It remains a movable municipal bridge and can be opened for boats by arrangement. [Gemeente Leiden](https://gemeente.leiden.nl/inwoners-en-ondernemers/sport-cultuur-en-recreatie/vrije-tijd/bedieningstijden-gemeentelijke-bruggen/?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
Its best role in the app is visual rather than biographical.
Do not imply that the surviving bridge is one Rembrandt personally crossed.
Instead, use the combination of mill, water and bridge to restore the atmosphere of Leiden's western edge.
Reconstruction as a historical story
De Put ultimately tells two stories.
One begins in 1619:
working mills, urban growth and food production.
The other begins in the twentieth century:
archaeology, heritage activism and reconstruction.
The modern structure therefore does something valuable.
It does not claim to have survived.
It makes something lost understandable again.

Eine größere Nachfolgerin
1729 wurde die Bockwindmühle durch eine größere Stellingmühle ersetzt.

Same architect, different gateway
The best way to understand the Zijlpoort is to compare it directly with the Morspoort.
Both belong to Leiden's major seventeenth-century expansion.
Both are associated with city architect Willem van der Helm.
They were built only a few years apart.
Yet they look strikingly different.
That was not a failure of consistency.
It was design.
1667
Historical architectural records state that the first stone was laid on 9 April 1667.
Willem van der Helm produced the design. [DBNL](https://www.dbnl.org/tekst/kuil005prov02_01/kuil005prov02_01_0010.php?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
Visit Leiden also dates the gate to 1667. [Visit Leiden](https://www.visitleiden.nl/nl/locaties/2311695342/zijlpoort?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
The date is therefore well supported.
The name
The Zijlpoort takes its name from the river Zijl. [Visit Leiden](https://www.visitleiden.nl/nl/locaties/2311695342/zijlpoort?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
It marked an entrance on Leiden's northeastern side.
Like Morspoort, the name preserves the geography that lay beyond the walls.
A difficult site
The gate had to connect with both a bridge and a defensive wall.
Unfortunately for the architect, those elements did not meet at a clean right angle.
Van der Helm's solution was elegant:
make the building itself skewed.
The plan became a parallelogram. [Visit Leiden](https://www.visitleiden.nl/nl/locaties/2311695342/zijlpoort?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
The irregular geometry was carefully continued through the structure.
Historic architectural descriptions even note that the octagonal tower was distorted to respond to the underlying axes. [DBNL](https://www.dbnl.org/tekst/kuil005prov02_01/kuil005prov02_01_0010.php?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
From street level, most visitors never notice.
That makes it an excellent digital interpretation point.
A useful AR sequence
Show the façade first.
It appears normal.
Then switch to an overhead plan.
Overlay the historic wall and bridge.
Suddenly the unusual geometry makes perfect sense.
This is precisely the kind of detail that a location-based app can explain more effectively than a conventional plaque.
Van der Helm
Willem van der Helm became Leiden's city architect in the 1660s. [DBNL](https://www.dbnl.org/tekst/molh003nieu01_01/molh003nieu01_01_1702.php?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
His gates were not identical repetitions.
Architectural research stresses that each received its own composition and symbolic programme. [DBNL](https://www.dbnl.org/tekst/_zev001200601_01/_zev001200601_01_0003.php?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
The Morspoort uses a deliberately Renaissance-inspired language.
The Zijlpoort is more overtly classicist.
Architecture as communication
City gates were perfect locations for visual propaganda.
Every traveller had to pass through them.
The architecture could therefore communicate power without requiring a written explanation.
The Zijlpoort's sculptural decoration was created by Rombout Verhulst. [Visit Leiden](https://www.visitleiden.nl/nl/locaties/2311695342/zijlpoort?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
Military trophies and symbols of civic authority appear around the gateway.
On the outside, Leiden's coat of arms is presented with lions and military imagery. [DBNL](https://www.dbnl.org/tekst/kuil005prov02_01/kuil005prov02_01_0010.php?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
The message was straightforward.
This is a governed city.
A defended city.
A city that expects to be taken seriously.
Medusa
Architectural historians identify a particularly dramatic motif on the city side:
a Medusa head with snakes. [DBNL](https://www.dbnl.org/tekst/_zev001200601_01/_zev001200601_01_0003.php?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
Medusa was the mythological figure whose gaze turned people to stone.
The image therefore functions as something more threatening than simple ornament.
Research compares the idea with Michelangelo's earlier Porta Pia in Rome, where a similarly intimidating mask was incorporated into a monumental city gate. [DBNL](https://www.dbnl.org/tekst/_zev001200601_01/_zev001200601_01_0003.php?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
Leiden's architecture was participating in an international Renaissance tradition.
The tower was not originally inevitable
One revealing construction detail is that the tower was added to the plan only after the project had already begun.
In June 1667, a decision was made to give the gateway a tower. [DBNL](https://www.dbnl.org/tekst/kuil005prov02_01/kuil005prov02_01_0010.php?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
A bell by Pieter Hemony, dated 1668, was later installed there. [DBNL](https://www.dbnl.org/tekst/kuil005prov02_01/kuil005prov02_01_0010.php?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
The gate therefore developed during construction rather than emerging from one completely fixed concept.
Rooms inside
Guard spaces sit beside the passage and a large room occupies the upper floor. [DBNL](https://www.dbnl.org/tekst/sten009monu10_01/sten009monu10_01_0084.php?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
An older architectural study suggests that the impressive height of the building may have been driven as much by representation as by practical necessity. [DBNL](https://www.dbnl.org/tekst/kuil005prov02_01/kuil005prov02_01_0010.php?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
The gate did not merely have to work.
It had to look important.
Rhetoricians
In the eighteenth century, rooms in the Zijlpoort were used by rederijkers — organised literary and theatrical societies. [Visit Leiden](https://www.visitleiden.nl/nl/locaties/2311695342/zijlpoort?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
A defensive gateway became a cultural meeting place.
School for poor children
From 1736, part of the building served for some years as a school for poor children. [DBNL](https://www.dbnl.org/tekst/kuil005prov02_01/kuil005prov02_01_0010.php?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
The contrast is striking.
Architecture created to display civic power became a place where some of the city's poorest children received education.
Municipal storage
The building also served for storing municipal supplies. [Visit Leiden](https://www.visitleiden.nl/nl/locaties/2311695342/zijlpoort?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
Less dramatic, perhaps.
But characteristic of the long life of urban monuments.
Once the original purpose disappears, useful spaces find new jobs.
The missing wall
Most of Leiden's defensive system disappeared.
The Zijlpoort and Morspoort survived as rare gateways. [DBNL](https://www.dbnl.org/tekst/sten009monu10_01/sten009monu10_01_0084.php?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
Today they can look strangely isolated because the walls that gave them meaning have vanished.
A good historical reconstruction should therefore restore the wall around the gate, not simply the building itself.
Harbour life today
The modern area beside the Zijlpoort contains Leiden's visitors' harbour, where boat travellers can stay overnight. [Visit Leiden](https://www.visitleiden.nl/nl/doen/varen/haven?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
The historical contrast is appealing.
The city once controlled entry here.
Today visitors arrive by boat and enter freely.
Visiting
The exterior and passage can be experienced freely.
The Zijlpoort does not operate as a standard daily museum.
Its strongest interpretive material is therefore available outside:
architecture,
geometry,
sculpture,
and the relationship to the vanished wall.

Then let visitors discover the difference.
Historical architecture becomes much easier to understand when tourists are encouraged to compare rather than simply memorise.
