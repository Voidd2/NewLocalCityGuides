For many American visitors, the familiar story begins with a ship.
The Mayflower.

Before Plymouth, there was Leiden.
The people we now call the Pilgrims were English religious Separatists. They believed the Church of England had not reformed far enough, and under King James I, separation from the established church could bring serious legal consequences. Some members of the community were arrested while trying to leave England. Eventually they escaped to the Dutch Republic, first settling in Amsterdam and then, in 1609, receiving permission to live here in Leiden.
Now imagine arriving here.
You speak English.
You come largely from rural communities.
And suddenly you are living in a crowded Dutch textile city.
Leiden gave the group more room to practice their faith, but religious freedom did not solve every problem. Many struggled economically. Their old skills did not always transfer easily to urban work. Some entered the textile industry and earned very little.
Meanwhile, their children were growing up.
Speaking Dutch.
Making Dutch friends.
Becoming part of Leiden.
For parents who had already sacrificed almost everything to preserve their religious community, that created another fear: what if their children eventually stopped being English at all?
Now look toward Kloksteeg 21.
In March 1611, John Robinson and three associates purchased property here at a site then known as the Groenepoort. Robinson became the pastor at the centre of the congregation, and English families lived around him. The English presence became so strongly associated with the site that it later acquired another name:
the English Gate.
And the community was not politically quiet.
Nearby, William Brewster operated a printing press in an alley then known as the Stinksteeg — today the William Brewstersteeg. Books attacking English religious policy were printed here and smuggled back across the North Sea. The press became serious enough to contribute to a diplomatic dispute between England and the Dutch Republic.
Then, after almost twelve years, part of the community chose to leave again.
This decision was not simply a romantic search for adventure.
Money was difficult. They worried about their children's identity. The political situation in Europe was uncertain. And they wanted to establish a community where they could shape religious and social life themselves.
They acquired a small ship, the Speedwell.
In 1620, a group left Leiden by water for Delfshaven. From there they sailed to England, where the Speedwell was supposed to cross the Atlantic alongside the Mayflower. The Speedwell repeatedly leaked, however, and was eventually abandoned. Some passengers returned home; others crowded onto the Mayflower.
One correction matters here.
The Mayflower did not carry 102 Leiden Pilgrims.
It carried 102 passengers in total, and fewer than half belonged to the Separatist group. The others were colonists whom the Separatists referred to as “Strangers.”
And John Robinson?
He never crossed the Atlantic.
He stayed in Leiden with those who remained behind and died here in 1625.

These people were refugees, workers, parents and neighbours in Leiden long before they became historical symbols.

De Engelse Poort
Een van de belangrijkste concrete plekken ligt aan de huidige Kloksteeg.

Historische Vereniging Oud Leiden laat daarmee iets belangrijks zien: de verbinding tussen Robinson en deze locatie is niet alleen een latere toeristische traditie. Ze staat in zeventiende-eeuwse eigendomsdocumenten.

The missing middle of the Mayflower story
Ask many Americans where the Pilgrim story begins and the answer will involve the Mayflower.
That skips almost twelve years.
Before a small group of English religious refugees became part of an American national origin story, they were immigrants in Leiden.
Their presence here is unusually well documented. Marriage certificates, property transactions, burial records, wills and court cases survive in Leiden's archives. These were not anonymous travellers waiting for their ship.
They built lives here.
Separatists, not yet “Pilgrim Fathers”
The group emerged from the religious conflicts following the English Reformation.
The monarch headed the Church of England. Some Protestants thought that church still retained too many Catholic practices.
Those who wanted to purify it from within became known broadly as Puritans.
Others believed true reform required leaving the established church altogether.
They were Separatists.
Under King James I, separation from the national church could carry severe consequences. Members of the Scrooby congregation attempted to escape England. One effort in 1607 ended in arrest before later attempts finally succeeded.
They reached the Dutch Republic.
Why Leiden?
The first destination was Amsterdam, which already contained English religious refugees.
Internal disputes eventually encouraged John Robinson's congregation to seek another home.
Robinson petitioned Leiden's authorities for permission to settle here. Around 1609, roughly one hundred men and women arrived.
Leiden offered advantages.
It was international.
It had a university.
Its textile industry created employment.
And compared with England, it gave the congregation considerably more space to organise its religious life.
But tolerance should not be confused with complete freedom or prosperity.
They could not simply establish a conventional public church of their own. The congregation frequently met at Robinson's property near the Pieterskerk.
The English Gate
At Kloksteeg 21, archival evidence takes us unusually close to individual people.
A deed dated 14 March 1611 records John Robinson and three associates purchasing property at a location known as the Groenepoort.
Robinson lived here and remained associated with this property until his death in 1625.
The concentration of English residents eventually caused the place to become known as the Engelse Poort — the English Gate.
The courtyard you see today is not unchanged Pilgrim architecture.
The present Jean Pesijnhof was founded later, in 1683.
That distinction matters because heritage becomes more interesting, not less, when its layers are kept separate.
The location is authentic.
The surviving courtyard belongs to a later chapter.
A difficult economic life
Most of the English newcomers had not been trained for life in a major textile city.
Work was a persistent problem.
Some entered Leiden's textile trades, but wages could be low, and the economic situation of many families remained difficult.
Meanwhile, their children adapted.
They learned Dutch.
They socialised in Leiden.
And their parents became increasingly worried that the community's English identity would disappear over another generation.
This gives their eventual departure a more human motive.
They were not simply chasing an abstract dream.
They were asking what kind of adults their children would become.
William Brewster's press
The community also remained connected to English politics.
William Brewster lived nearby and was involved with a printing operation in the alley now named after him.
In the early seventeenth century it was called Stinksteeg.
Texts hostile to English religious policy were produced there and smuggled back into England. The operation became serious enough to create a diplomatic dispute.
Stand in that narrow alley today and the scale feels almost absurd.
International controversy emerged from a space small enough to walk through in seconds.
Deciding to leave Leiden
By the late 1610s the congregation was considering another migration.
Economics played a role.
The assimilation of their children played a role.
The approaching end of the Twelve Years' Truce raised concerns about renewed war.
Most importantly, members hoped to establish a community where they could control more of their religious and civic life.
The plan eventually focused on North America.
Leaving by water
The Leiden congregation purchased the Speedwell.
In 1620 those leaving travelled from Leiden along the Vliet toward Delfshaven. A modern monument along the Vliet commemorates that departure.
From Delfshaven, the Speedwell sailed to England and joined the Mayflower.
The story nearly collapsed there.
The Speedwell repeatedly took on water. After two failed attempts to cross the Atlantic, it was declared unsuitable. Some travellers abandoned the voyage.
Others squeezed aboard the Mayflower.
When that vessel finally sailed from Plymouth on 16 September 1620, it carried 102 passengers.
Fewer than half were members of the Separatist congregation.
The others were colonists whom the Separatists called “Strangers.”
That correction matters.
The Mayflower passengers were not one homogeneous religious group.
John Robinson stayed behind
Robinson never boarded the Mayflower.
Many members of the Leiden congregation remained in Europe, and there was an expectation that others might follow later.
Robinson continued leading those who remained.
He died in Leiden in 1625 and was buried in association with the Pieterskerk.
For an American visitor, that changes the emotional geography of the story.
One of the central spiritual leaders of the Pilgrim congregation never saw America.
His final city was Leiden.
Beyond the national myth
The Atlantic crossing led the colonists into the territory of the Wampanoag.
They did not arrive in an empty wilderness. Indigenous communities had occupied the region for generations, while epidemics linked to earlier European contact had recently caused catastrophic population losses.
That later colonial history is larger than this location.
But acknowledging it matters.
It keeps the Leiden chapter from becoming merely the prologue to a triumphant national myth.
These were real migrants making uncertain decisions.
They could not know what the Mayflower would later represent.
Standing here, neither can you see the future.
You can only see the place they once called home.

What exactly is a hofje?
Foreign visitors often need one Dutch word explained before this place makes sense:
hofje.
The closest English expression is usually almshouse courtyard, but that translation is imperfect.
A hofje is generally a small group of homes built around a shared courtyard and funded through a charitable foundation. Historic hofjes often provided accommodation for people with limited income: elderly residents, widows, unmarried women or members of a particular religious community.
Each foundation had its own rules.
In modern terms, you might think of it as a small form of privately endowed social housing.
The English community came first
The Jean Pesijnhof stands on a site with an earlier identity.
It was known as the Groenepoort.
On 14 March 1611, John Robinson and three associates from his English religious community purchased property here.
Their presence eventually gave the location a new name:
the English Gate.
Robinson lived here until he died in 1625.
Later records reveal that the site developed into a densely inhabited courtyard. By 1647, documents refer to 22 small houses, one of which was subsequently demolished.
So the courtyard you see today has a physical predecessor, although the present institution belongs to a later story.
Jean Pesijn and Marie de Lannoy
Jean Pesijn came from the region around Lille in what is now northern France.
He and his wife, Marie de Lannoy, belonged to Leiden's French-speaking Protestant — or Walloon Reformed — community.
“Walloon” in this historical setting refers broadly to French-speaking Reformed migrants from the southern Low Countries and neighbouring regions of northern France.
Leiden attracted many such refugees.
War, religious conflict and economic opportunity had transformed the city into a place where migrants became a significant part of urban life.
Pesijn and De Lannoy arranged for their wealth to provide homes for poor elderly members of their community.
Historical research records Jean's death in 1666 and Marie's in 1681. Their testamentary executors then acquired the necessary property and created twelve houses.
The gateway is dated 1683.
Was it created out of grief?
One popular version gives the foundation a particularly emotional origin.
The Leiden Hofjes Festival states that Jean and Marie lost their only daughter during the plague epidemic of 1655, and that this loss contributed to their decision to establish an almshouse.
It is a plausible and meaningful tradition.
However, the archival sources retrieved for this project establish the charitable foundation more securely than they establish grief as the exact motivation.

AANVULLENDE FEITEN

* Exacte datum: 12 januari 1807, explosie rond 16.15 uur.
* Hoeveelheid buskruit: circa 37.000 Hollandse ponden, ongeveer 17,8 ton. Een aantal moderne toeristische pagina's schrijft ten onrechte of vereenvoudigd “37 ton”; voor de app zou ik 17,8 ton gebruiken.
* Slachtoffers: voor de app 151 doden, circa 2.000 gewonden. Andere bronnen geven afgerond circa 160 of 161 doden; vermeld die bronvariatie eventueel in de achtergrondinformatie.
* Oorzaak: onbekend. Verhalen over koken/vuur aan boord zijn hypothesen, geen vastgesteld feit.
* Huidige functie: een deel van het voormalige rampgebied vormt nu het Van der Werfpark; aan de overzijde staat onder meer het Kamerlingh Onnes Gebouw.
* Openbaar toegankelijk: ja, het park is gratis openbaar toegankelijk.
* Beste moment om te bezoeken: overdag, zodat de gedenksteen aan de kade en de ruimtelijke relatie tussen park, Steenschuur en omliggende gebouwen goed zichtbaar zijn.
* Leuk weetje dat niet in het hoofdverhaal staat: de explosie beschadigde ook gebouwen buiten het directe rampgebied; zelfs de Leidse synagoge liep zware schade op en verloor een deel van haar archief.
* Connectie met andere locaties in de route: L039 Rapenburg, omdat Steenschuur in het verlengde daarvan ligt; L032 Oude Sterrewacht en andere universitaire plekken voor het verhaal van Leiden als kennisstad; het huidige Kamerlingh Onnes Gebouw laat zien hoe wetenschap later letterlijk op een deel van het rampterrein terechtkwam.
