## 1. WHAT THIS PLACE WAS — the short answer

An artificial hill, built by hand, at the fork of the Rhine, because whoever held that fork
held everything that moved through it. Leiden grew around the hill, not the other way round.

---

## 2. THE PLACE (master §9 — Place)

### What was it?
A **motte**: an artificial mound carrying a defensive structure. On top stands a stone **ring
wall** with a **weergang** (wall-walk) and — originally — a **tower that no longer exists**.
It is described as one of the best-preserved motte castles in the Netherlands.

### When was it created?
- **Late 9th century** — excavated timber structures in the mound date from the end of the
  9th and beginning of the 10th century. The mound was raised with **plaggen (sods) and clay**.
- **In phases** — the hill was heightened repeatedly.
- **c. 1150** — the mound reached its present size and height.
- **2nd half of the 13th century** — the circular stone wall (see `R05`: attribution to
  Floris V is *possible*, not established).

### What changed over time?
| When | What changed |
|---|---|
| Late 9th c. → c. 1150 | Earth mound raised in phases, sods and clay |
| Until the 12th c. | Control **disputed between the count of Holland and the bishop of Utrecht** |
| **1206** | Falls definitively to the count of Holland |
| **c. 1350** | **Loses its military function** — the city walls of Leiden now enclose it |
| **1651** | The city of Leiden buys the Burcht from the burgrave |
| **1658–59** | New entrance gate built |
| **1662** | Lion sculpture placed on the gate |
| 17th c. onward | Grounds become a **public park**, with a lodging house for wealthy cloth merchants |
| **1968** | Ring wall listed as a built rijksmonument |
| Recent | **The mound itself** designated an **archaeological** rijksmonument |

### Which parts survive? What no longer exists?
**Survives:** the mound, the ring wall, the wall-walk, the 1658–59 entrance gate with its two
crenellated towers, the city arms, and the 1662 lion.
**Gone:** the tower that once stood inside the ring wall. The medieval interior buildings.

## 3. THE PEOPLE (master §9 — People)

- **The count of Holland and the bishop of Utrecht** — rival claimants until the 12th century.
  The Burcht is a physical artefact of that rivalry.
- **Claude-Lamoral de Ligne**, burgrave, **sold the Burcht to the city of Leiden**, which then
  commissioned the new entrance gate. *(Confirm the sale date against the 1651 purchase — see `R05`.)*
- **Willem van der Helm** — built the gate, 1658–59. Also the architect of the **Morspoort**
  (C011) and the **Zijlpoort** (C012).
- **Van Noort**, city mason — the gate's design.
- **Pieter Post** — modified the design in **July 1658**.
- **Rombout Verhulst** — designed the **lion** (1662). Also the Zijlpoort sculpture (C012) and
  the Pesthuis relief (C028).
- **Wealthy cloth merchants** — the 17th-century lodging house on the grounds was for them.
  The Burcht's afterlife is a *textile* story (→ L013).

## 4. THE ACTION — what physically happened here (master §9 — Action)

**Early:** the mound served as a **refuge in times of danger** — including, specifically, from
the **regularly rising water of the Rhine**. People and **their livestock** sheltered behind
the palisade and later the ring wall.

**Medieval:** a military and seigneurial strongpoint controlling the river fork.

**After c. 1350:** enclosed by the city, militarily pointless, and slowly decaying.

**From the 17th century:** a **pleasure ground**. This is the most surprising transition in the
location and it is documented: a fortification became a place to stroll, with a lodging house
for rich merchants.

### The water system — the best-documented physical detail here
From a **water cellar low in the mound**, water was pumped up to **two reservoirs at the top of
the Burcht**. From there, pipes ran:
- to a **fish fountain on the other side of the Nieuwe Rijn**, and
- to a **fountain at the foot of the Burcht**.

**This is a direct physical link between L001 and L002.** The hill was not only a fortress; it
was the city's water tower, and it fed the fish market below. Two consecutive stops on the
route, joined by a pipe. *(See `R05` — dating of the system must be established.)*

## 5. THE ENVIRONMENT (master §9 — Environment)

- **Water is the whole point.** The mound sits where the Oude Rijn and Nieuwe Rijn meet.
- The earliest function was as much about **flood** as about attack — refuge from rising water.
- After c. 1350 the surroundings are **urban**, not open: the city has grown around and past it.
- Materials: **sods and clay** (mound), **brick/stone** (ring wall), **sandstone** sculpture
  (gate, lion).

## 6. THE NARRATIVE — the one story someone will remember

**Candidate:** *"This hill is not a hill."*

Everything follows from that: it was carried here, basket by basket, in sods and clay, over
roughly 250 years, because two rivers meet at this spot. The city is downstream of that
decision in every sense.

- **Conflict:** count versus bishop, and later the city buying out its own castle.
- **Transformation:** fortress → obsolete → ruin → public park → protected monument. Four
  complete changes of meaning for one pile of earth.
- **Human perspective:** people and animals sheltering from the river, not from armies.
- **Unexpected detail:** the fortress was also a **water tower** feeding a fountain at the fish
  market across the river.

## 9. R05 — UNCERTAINTY REGISTER (master §8)

Separated as master requires. **Nothing in "Probable", "Plausible" or "Disputed" may be stated
as fact in visitor content.**

### VERIFIED (multiple independent institutional sources, this pass)
- Timber structures in the mound date from the **end of the 9th / beginning of the 10th century**.
- The mound was raised with **sods and clay**, in phases, reaching present size **c. 1150**.
- Control was **disputed between the count of Holland and the bishop of Utrecht**; from **1206**
  it was definitively the count's.
- The ring wall is a **rijksmonument since 1968**; the mound is an **archaeological rijksmonument**.
- The entrance gate dates from **1658–59**; **Willem van der Helm** built it, to a design by city
  mason **Van Noort**, modified by **Pieter Post** in July 1658.
- The **lion** on the gate dates from **1662**, to a design by **Rombout Verhulst**.
- The city **bought the Burcht in 1651**.
- The Burcht **lost its military function around 1350** when the city walls enclosed it.

### PROBABLE (widely stated, single-source in this pass — confirm in `R02`)
- The **water system**: cellar → two reservoirs → pipes → fish fountain across the Nieuwe Rijn
  and a fountain at the foot. **Dating unknown.** Almost certainly post-1651, but that is an
  inference, not a source.
- That the grounds included a **lodging house for wealthy cloth merchants**.
- The sale by **Claude-Lamoral de Ligne** — needs reconciling with the 1651 purchase date.

### PLAUSIBLE (do not publish)
- The stone ring wall built **under Floris V**. Stated as *possible* by the sources, not established.

### DISPUTED / UNRESOLVED
- **Who built the original mound** — count of Holland or bishop of Utrecht. The sources say the
  two disputed it. **The product must say we do not know**, which is the more interesting answer.

### ⚠️ LEGEND — MUST NOT BE PRESENTED AS HISTORY (master §11)
- That during the **1574 siege** townspeople sheltered on the Burcht. Sources phrase this as
  *"het gerucht gaat"* — **a rumour**.
- That a **secret passage from the well** let people get behind the Spanish lines. Explicitly
  **a legend**.

These are attractive and will be tempting. If used at all, they must be labelled as the stories
Leiden tells about itself — never as what happened. **DEC-013 and master §11 apply.**
