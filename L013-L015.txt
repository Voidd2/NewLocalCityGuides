LEESTEKST (EN)
An industrial machine disguised as an icon
Windmills have become such powerful symbols of the Netherlands that visitors can easily forget their original purpose.
They were machines.
A grain mill converted wind into mechanical work.
The sails turned an axle.
Gears transferred rotation through the building.
Millstones ground grain into flour.
Flour became bread.
De Valk was therefore part of Leiden's food infrastructure.
Nineteen mills
The museum's official history records that nineteen windmills once stood on Leiden's city fortifications.
De Valk is the surviving original city-wall mill from that landscape. [Molenmuseum De Valk](https://molenmuseumdevalk.nl/over-het-museum/geschiedenis?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
The location was practical.
Windmills need wind.
Dense urban streets create turbulence and shelter.
Elevated defensive walls offered much better exposure.
The fortifications therefore doubled as an industrial zone.
The first De Valck
The present stone mill was not the first one here.
In 1611, a wooden post mill called De Valck was built on the Valkenburger bastion.
In 1667, it was replaced by a larger wooden tower mill. [Molenmuseum De Valk](https://molenmuseumdevalk.nl/over-het-museum/geschiedenis?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
As Leiden became more densely built, height became increasingly important.
A city windmill had to reach above its surroundings.
The stone mill of 1743
In 1743, the city authorities granted permission for a larger stone mill.
That is the structure visitors see today.
Its brick body was reportedly constructed in only about three months.
The mill reaches approximately 29 metres and originally contained four pairs of millstones, giving it substantial capacity. [Molenmuseum De Valk](https://molenmuseumdevalk.nl/over-het-museum/geschiedenis?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
Not every component was new.
A large gear inside bears the date 1697, showing that older machinery was reused when the new mill was built. [Molenmuseum De Valk](https://molenmuseumdevalk.nl/collectie/uitgelicht?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
Industrial archaeology often reveals exactly this kind of practical behaviour.
If a component still worked, use it.
Living beneath the machinery
De Valk was not only a workplace.
People lived inside it.
The building originally incorporated space for two households.
Kitchens, living rooms and a stable occupied lower spaces.
Bedrooms were located on the first and second floors.
Storage took up the third.
Only on the fourth level, approximately fourteen metres above ground, did the main milling machinery begin. [Molenmuseum De Valk](https://www.molenmuseumdevalk.nl/ontdek-de-molen?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
The arrangement is remarkable.
Family life below.
Industry above.
A machine and a home occupying the same tower.
The Van Rhijn family
In 1869, miller Pieter van Rhijn bought De Valk.
He belonged to a South Holland milling family.
During his ownership, the two residential sections were converted into one larger miller's home.
Much of that interior survives. [Molenmuseum De Valk](https://molenmuseumdevalk.nl/over-het-museum/geschiedenis?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
After Pieter died in 1889, his widow Jacobina Aleyda Schippers continued the business.
Their son Willem later trained as a miller and took control in 1911. [Molenmuseum De Valk](https://molenmuseumdevalk.nl/over-het-museum/geschiedenis?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
A supposed Rembrandt connection
Willem van Rhijn was fascinated by genealogy.
He investigated whether his own Van Rhijn milling family might have been related to the family of Rembrandt van Rijn, whose father had also been a Leiden miller.
He found no evidence. [Molenmuseum De Valk](https://molenmuseumdevalk.nl/over-het-museum/geschiedenis?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
That failed investigation is more interesting than inventing a connection.
It shows how people living inside the monument themselves tried to connect their family history with Leiden's famous past.
Wind loses to motors
By the late nineteenth century, traditional wind milling faced growing competition.
Leiden's population had declined and powered milling installations could operate regardless of weather.
Many city mills were demolished.
De Valk nevertheless continued producing flour well into the twentieth century, although its final working years no longer depended exclusively on wind. [Molenmuseum De Valk](https://molenmuseumdevalk.nl/over-het-museum/geschiedenis?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
The transition is a classic Industrial Revolution story.
Wind power did not disappear because it stopped working.
It became commercially less competitive.
War
On 12 May 1940, De Valk was temporarily turned into an observation post for Dutch troops.
Two days later the Netherlands capitulated.
During fighting, the mill had been fired upon from the air, leaving bullet damage in the structure. [Molenmuseum De Valk](https://www.molenmuseumdevalk.nl/over-het-museum/geschiedenis/page%3A2)
Under occupation, grain milling remained essential.
Food became rationed.
After 1943, wheat grew increasingly scarce.
During the Hunger Winter of 1944–45, residents sometimes brought private grain to the mill illegally.
The museum records that grain was also milled for the resistance and that food supplies were hidden in the building. [Molenmuseum De Valk](https://www.molenmuseumdevalk.nl/over-het-museum/geschiedenis/page%3A2)
A mill partly burned for fuel
Fuel shortages produced another kind of damage.
Wooden parts of the windmill were removed and burned.
By the end of the war, De Valk was in poor condition. [Molenmuseum De Valk](https://www.molenmuseumdevalk.nl/over-het-museum/geschiedenis/page%3A2)
The municipality funded major restoration work in 1947 because the historical significance of the structure had already been recognised. [Molenmuseum De Valk](https://www.molenmuseumdevalk.nl/over-het-museum/geschiedenis/page%3A2)
The windmill therefore survived not through luck alone.
It survived because someone decided to invest in it.
The last miller becomes the first guide
Willem van Rhijn remained an enthusiastic resident.
Long before the mill officially became a museum, he welcomed visitors.
The museum still possesses a guest book with signatures going back to 1930.
He particularly enjoyed showing foreign visitors around. [Molenmuseum De Valk](https://www.molenmuseumdevalk.nl/over-het-museum/geschiedenis/page%3A3)
In a way, he became De Valk's first tour guide.
Willem died in 1964, aged 87.
On 2 June 1966, the windmill officially opened as a municipal museum. [Molenmuseum De Valk](https://www.molenmuseumdevalk.nl/over-het-museum/geschiedenis/page%3A3)
A machine again
More recently, the mill has been made capable of grinding again.
That makes the museum experience much stronger.
Static gears can be difficult to understand.
Moving gears explain themselves.
The moment power moves through the building, visitors can see how wind becomes flour. [Molenmuseum De Valk](https://www.molenmuseumdevalk.nl/over-het-museum/geschiedenis/page%3A3)
Reconstruct the old skyline
The outdoor gallery sits at approximately 14 metres and provides a broad view across Leiden. [Molenmuseum De Valk](https://molenmuseumdevalk.nl/collectie/uitgelicht?utm_source=chatgpt.com)
This is an ideal location for an augmented-reality layer.
Show the modern skyline.
Then reintroduce the vanished city-wall windmills one by one.
Suddenly, De Valk is no longer a lone picturesque survivor.
It becomes a fragment of an industrial landscape.
